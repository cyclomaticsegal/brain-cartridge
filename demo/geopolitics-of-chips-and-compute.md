# The Geopolitics of Chips: How Semiconductor Supply Chains Shape AI Power

The global AI race is ultimately a hardware race, and the hardware race is a geopolitical story. The most advanced AI chips in the world (the GPUs and custom accelerators that train frontier models) depend on a supply chain of extraordinary fragility. Understanding this supply chain is essential to understanding who controls the future of AI.

## The TSMC Dependency

Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company fabricates roughly 90% of the world's most advanced chips. Not designs; fabrication. NVIDIA designs its GPUs, but TSMC builds them. Apple, Qualcomm, AMD, and most AI chip startups are in the same position. They are "fabless" (they design chips but depend entirely on TSMC's foundries to manufacture them).

This creates a single point of failure for the entire AI industry. TSMC's most advanced facilities are concentrated in a handful of sites in Taiwan, an island that exists in a state of geopolitical tension with China. A disruption to TSMC's operations (whether from military conflict, natural disaster, or political coercion) would halt frontier AI development globally for years.

The strategic implications are not lost on anyone. The United States, through the CHIPS Act and export controls, is simultaneously trying to build domestic chip fabrication capacity and restrict China's access to advanced chips. TSMC is building new fabs in Arizona and Japan, but these facilities won't reach full capacity until the late 2020s and will still produce a fraction of what Taiwan delivers.

## US-China Chip Controls

Since October 2022, the United States has imposed increasingly aggressive export controls on advanced semiconductors and chip-making equipment to China. The initial controls targeted chips above a certain performance threshold and the lithography equipment needed to manufacture them. Subsequent rounds have tightened loopholes, restricted third-country re-exports, and targeted specific Chinese companies.

The impact has been significant but not decisive. China's leading AI labs (including DeepSeek, Baidu, and Alibaba) have adapted by hoarding pre-ban chips, developing workarounds using clusters of less-advanced hardware, and investing heavily in domestic chip design. Huawei's Ascend 910B represents China's most advanced domestic AI accelerator, competitive with NVIDIA's A100 but still a generation behind the H100 and B200.

The deeper question is whether export controls can maintain a lasting capability gap or merely delay the inevitable. History suggests that technology denial strategies have a half-life. They buy time (perhaps five to ten years) but they also incentivise the target country to build indigenous capabilities it might not otherwise have prioritised. China's semiconductor investment has accelerated dramatically since the controls began.

## Sovereign AI and Compute Nationalism

A new concept has entered the geopolitical vocabulary: sovereign AI. Countries from France to Saudi Arabia to India are declaring that they need domestic AI capabilities (models trained on their languages and data, running on infrastructure they control). This isn't just nationalism. It reflects a legitimate concern that dependence on American AI platforms creates strategic vulnerability.

The sovereign AI movement is driving a global buildout of data centre capacity. The UAE has invested tens of billions in GPU clusters. The European Union's AI Act is paired with funding for European compute infrastructure. Japan and South Korea are subsidising domestic chip fabrication. Even mid-sized economies are realising that AI capability requires physical infrastructure, not just software talent.

This compute nationalism is reshaping capital flows. Countries that can offer cheap energy, political stability, and proximity to undersea cable routes are attracting massive data centre investment. The Middle East, Scandinavia, and parts of Southeast Asia are emerging as new hubs. The geography of AI power is being redrawn along the lines of energy availability and infrastructure investment, not traditional measures of technological sophistication.

## The Compute OPEC Hypothesis

Some analysts have drawn parallels between compute and oil. Both are essential inputs to economic activity. Both have concentrated supply chains. Both create geopolitical leverage for those who control them. If compute becomes the critical resource of the 21st-century economy (as energy was for the 20th), then control of chip fabrication, data centre capacity, and the energy to power them becomes a source of strategic power comparable to control of oil reserves.

The analogy is imperfect but instructive. Unlike oil, compute can be manufactured rather than extracted. But the manufacturing requires capabilities (extreme ultraviolet lithography from ASML, advanced packaging from TSMC, high-bandwidth memory from SK Hynix) that are as geographically concentrated as any oil field. The countries and companies that control these chokepoints will wield enormous influence over the trajectory of AI development worldwide.
