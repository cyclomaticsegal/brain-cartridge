# AI Regulation: The Global Governance Patchwork

There is no unified global framework for governing AI. What exists instead is a patchwork of national and regional approaches, each reflecting different political priorities, economic interests, and cultural attitudes toward technology and risk. Understanding this patchwork matters because regulation shapes where AI gets built, how it gets deployed, and who captures the economic value.

## The European Approach: Regulate First

The European Union's AI Act, which entered force in stages from 2024 through 2026, represents the most comprehensive attempt to regulate AI through legislation. It classifies AI systems by risk level (unacceptable, high, limited, and minimal) and imposes requirements proportional to that classification. High-risk systems (used in hiring, credit scoring, law enforcement, critical infrastructure) must meet standards for transparency, human oversight, data quality, and robustness.

For frontier model developers, the AI Act introduces specific obligations for "general-purpose AI" systems. Labs training models above a certain compute threshold must conduct safety evaluations, report serious incidents, document training data, and comply with copyright obligations. These requirements apply regardless of where the model was trained. If it's offered in the EU market, it must comply.

The practical effect has been mixed. Large US and Chinese labs have compliance teams dedicated to the EU market. Smaller players may choose not to serve Europe, creating a fragmentation in AI availability. Critics argue the Act regulates capabilities rather than harms, risks stifling innovation, and creates compliance costs that favour incumbents over startups. Supporters counter that establishing rules early prevents a race to the bottom and gives European citizens protections that market forces alone won't deliver.

## The American Approach: Industry-Led with Guardrails

The United States has taken a lighter regulatory approach, emphasising voluntary commitments, executive orders, and sector-specific guidance rather than comprehensive legislation. The Biden-era Executive Order on AI Safety (October 2023) established reporting requirements for frontier model training and directed agencies to develop sector-specific guidelines. Subsequent administrations have adjusted the emphasis but maintained the basic structure: encourage innovation, manage risks through existing regulatory frameworks, and avoid prescriptive rules that might disadvantage American companies.

The result is that the US remains the most permissive environment for AI development among major economies. This has contributed to American dominance in frontier AI; every leading Western lab is headquartered in the US. But it also means that deployment-side protections are weaker. AI systems are being deployed in hiring, healthcare, financial services, and law enforcement with less regulatory scrutiny than equivalent systems would face in Europe.

State-level regulation is filling some gaps. California, Colorado, and others have passed or proposed AI-related legislation. This creates its own complexity: companies deploying AI nationally must navigate a patchwork of state requirements, not unlike the situation with data privacy before and after CCPA.

## China: Strategic Control

China's approach to AI regulation is inseparable from its broader technology strategy. The government views AI as a strategic national asset and regulates accordingly: promoting development in areas that serve state interests while restricting applications that could threaten social stability or CCP authority.

China has published specific regulations for recommendation algorithms, deepfakes, and generative AI. These rules require that AI-generated content adheres to "core socialist values," that users can opt out of algorithmic recommendations, and that generative AI services undergo security assessments before public deployment. Enforcement is selective but real; companies that cross political red lines face swift consequences.

The net effect is an AI ecosystem that is technically sophisticated but constrained in application. Chinese AI labs produce world-class research and competitive models, but the deployment environment is shaped by political considerations that don't exist in Western markets. For international observers, the key question is whether this model of state-directed AI development can sustain innovation over the long term, or whether the constraints eventually slow progress relative to more open systems.

## The Governance Gap

The most significant regulatory challenge is the gap between the pace of AI development and the pace of governance. By the time a regulation is drafted, debated, and enacted, the technology has often moved on. The EU AI Act was negotiated over three years (an eternity in AI development terms). Foundation models, which barely existed when the Act was first proposed, had to be retrofit into the framework late in the process.

International coordination is even slower. The OECD AI Principles, the G7 Hiroshima Process, and various UN initiatives have produced high-level statements of intent but little operational governance. There is no equivalent of the IAEA for AI; no international body has the mandate or capability to monitor frontier AI development, verify safety claims, or enforce standards.

This governance gap creates both risk and opportunity. Risk, because the most consequential AI systems are being deployed without adequate oversight. Opportunity, because the governance frameworks that eventually emerge will be shaped by whoever shows up with workable proposals. For consultants, analysts, and strategists, understanding both the technology and the regulatory landscape is becoming a core competency.
