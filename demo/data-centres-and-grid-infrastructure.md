# Data Centres and Grid Infrastructure: The Physical Layer of the AI Economy

AI is software, but it runs on hardware, and that hardware sits in buildings that consume enormous amounts of electricity and generate enormous amounts of heat. The data centre industry — once a boring backwater of real estate and facilities management — has become one of the most strategically important sectors in the global economy. Understanding the physical infrastructure layer is essential to understanding the constraints and opportunities in AI.

## The Hyperscaler Buildout

The five largest data centre operators — Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud, Meta, and Oracle — are collectively spending over $200 billion per year on capital expenditure, much of it on data centre construction and GPU procurement. This is an unprecedented level of infrastructure investment, comparable in scale to the buildout of the transcontinental railroads or the interstate highway system.

Microsoft alone has announced plans to spend $80 billion on AI-enabled data centres in fiscal year 2025. Google has committed $75 billion. These aren't aspirational figures — they're backed by signed contracts with NVIDIA for GPU supply, with utilities for power delivery, and with construction firms for building the facilities.

The physical scale is staggering. A modern hyperscale data centre campus can span millions of square feet, consume hundreds of megawatts, and require its own electrical substation. The largest facilities under construction in 2026 are designed for gigawatt-scale power delivery — levels previously associated with major industrial installations or small cities.

## Cooling: The Hidden Challenge

GPUs generate heat. A lot of heat. An NVIDIA H100 GPU has a thermal design power of 700 watts. A rack of eight H100s generates as much heat as a large residential furnace. A data centre with tens of thousands of GPUs generates heat equivalent to a small power plant — which, in thermodynamic terms, it is. The energy goes in as electricity and comes out as waste heat.

Traditional air cooling — the approach used in most data centres built before 2020 — is reaching its limits for AI workloads. The power density of GPU racks is simply too high for air to carry away the heat efficiently. The industry is transitioning to liquid cooling: running water or specialised coolant directly to the chip or through cold plates attached to the GPU modules.

Direct liquid cooling reduces energy overhead significantly. Air-cooled data centres typically spend 30-40% of their total energy on cooling (measured as Power Usage Effectiveness, or PUE, of 1.3-1.4). Liquid-cooled facilities can achieve PUE below 1.1, meaning almost all electricity goes to useful computation rather than thermal management. At the scale of modern AI data centres, this efficiency improvement saves hundreds of millions of dollars per year in operating costs.

The transition to liquid cooling requires a complete rethink of data centre design. Plumbing replaces ductwork. Leak detection becomes critical — water and electronics don't mix well. Maintenance procedures change fundamentally. New facilities are being designed from the ground up for liquid cooling, while older facilities face expensive retrofits or obsolescence.

## The Grid Connection Bottleneck

Even with unlimited capital, you can't build a data centre faster than you can get it connected to the electrical grid. Grid interconnection has become the binding constraint on AI infrastructure expansion in many markets.

The problem is straightforward: electrical grids were built for the loads of the 20th century — factories, homes, offices, retail. They weren't designed for the concentrated, high-density loads that modern data centres represent. Connecting a 200 MW data centre to the grid requires substations, transmission lines, and often generation capacity that doesn't yet exist.

In Northern Virginia — the epicentre of global data centre development — interconnection queues now stretch three to five years. Dominion Energy, the local utility, is building new transmission infrastructure as fast as regulators will allow, but demand is outpacing construction. Similar bottlenecks are emerging in Dublin, Amsterdam, Singapore, and Tokyo.

This has created a secondary market in "powered land" — sites that already have grid connections with sufficient capacity for data centre development. These sites command enormous premiums. Some data centre developers are signing power purchase agreements directly with generators — solar farms, wind installations, even nuclear plants — to bypass the congested grid entirely.

## The Investment Thesis

The data centre buildout represents one of the largest infrastructure investment cycles in history. For investors and strategists, the question is not whether this investment will happen — it's already underway — but where the value accrues.

The most obvious beneficiaries are the hyperscalers themselves, GPU manufacturers (primarily NVIDIA), and power companies in markets with data centre demand. But the secondary effects ripple through the economy: construction firms, electrical equipment manufacturers, cooling technology companies, fibre optic suppliers, and the real estate developers who control land with grid access.

The less obvious opportunity is in the intersection of energy and compute. Companies that can deliver reliable, cheap, clean power directly to AI workloads — whether through nuclear, geothermal, or innovative grid solutions — are solving what may be the most important supply-side constraint in the AI economy. Intelligence per unit of energy is the metric that matters, and whoever improves that ratio captures value at every layer of the stack.
